HR — Leave & Attendance Rules How the module decides what somebody was doing on a given day, and what they have left. A reference for anyone maintaining or extending it. Last updated 1 September 2026.

This is the ERP sub-module that replaced the UAE Excel leave tracker (UAE_Employee_Attendance_Leave_Tracker_2026 v5.1). That workbook remains the business-logic reference: where this document and the workbook disagree, the workbook wins — except in the two places noted below, where the workbook was found to be wrong and the owner ruled otherwise.

1. The one idea the whole module rests on

Only exceptions are stored. An ordinary working day has no row in the database at all. The system derives it.

The numbers are why. A year for twenty-three people is 9,125 day-cells, and of those 943 carried information. The other 8,182 were the word "Present" repeated. Storing them would have been storing a fact nobody had decided.

Everything downstream follows from this. There is no "attendance table" to read a day out of; there is a resolver that answers what a day was, and it is the same resolver for the grid on screen, the balance engine, the CSV export and the reports.

Why one resolver and not several. Two implementations of "was she at work on the 3rd" will eventually disagree, and the disagreement surfaces as a payroll figure that nobody can trace. So the precedence lives in exactly one function and every surface calls it.
Source: src/hr/lib/hr-effective-status.ts

2. Precedence — how a day is decided

Given a date and a person, the answer is the first of these that applies. Order is the whole rule.

1Outside employment — before joining, or after the last working day. Answers NOT_EMPLOYED; nothing is counted and nothing is shown.
2An explicit entry — somebody recorded a code for that person on that day. A human decision always beats a derivation.
3A standing rule whose code covers calendar days — a company shutdown booked as annual leave speaks for the weekend inside it, because that is how the same block would be charged if it were dragged on the grid. See §6.
4A public holiday — PH.
5A weekend — OFF. Saturday and Sunday by default, configurable per organisation because some GCC entities run Friday and Saturday.
6A standing rule that describes working — a permanently remote employee's WFH. It falls to here on purpose: a working arrangement cannot turn a Saturday into a working day.
7Otherwise — P, present.
Step 3 versus step 6 is the subtle one. Both are standing rules. What separates them is whether the rule's code describes time not worked (annual leave, sick, a shutdown) or working (present, work-from-home, on-duty). Time not worked outranks the calendar; working does not. That is what makes a company-wide shutdown charge the permanently remote employee too, while her WFH arrangement still stops at working days.
Owner ruling, 31 August 2026: "the shutdown wins."

The naming trap. Do not "tidy" this. -HD means half a day. SL-H means half pay. They differ by one character and they are different things: SL-HD is half a day charged to the full-pay tier; SL-H is a whole day charged to the half-pay tier. Renaming either for consistency would silently re-price somebody's sick leave.
Why paid is a coarse boolean. SL-H is recorded as paid, because the rate lives in its category (SICK_HALF) where the tier maths can read it. Squeezing a pay rate into a boolean is how a payroll figure ends up wrong.

4. Comp-off — how it is earned

Working both days of the same weekend earns exactly one comp-off. A lone Saturday earns none. Two working days marked OD earn none.

The mechanism generalises rather than hardcoding Saturday and Sunday. A weekend block is a maximal run of consecutive weekend days. A block earns one comp-off when every day in it is OD. For a two-day weekend that is exactly the ruling; for a one-day or three-day weekend it stays sensible.

Sat OD + Sun OD          → 1 comp-off
Sat OD + Sun (nothing)   → 0        a weekend half-worked is a weekend not given back
Wed OD + Thu OD          → 0        not a weekend
Sat OD + Sun OD + Sat OD + Sun OD (two weekends) → 2
The workbook computes this differently, and it is wrong. Its formula asks "was yesterday also OD?": 
IF(AND(F="OD", COUNTIFS(sameEmp, date-1, "OD") > 0), 1, 0)
That agrees on every Saturday-plus-Sunday pair, which is the overwhelming majority of real data, so the difference is invisible until something else happens. Across 58 on-duty days for 16 employees, exactly one row diverges: EMP002 worked Wednesday 14 and Thursday 15 January 2026 and was credited a comp-off this rule does not award. The one-time import therefore expects that single variance rather than treating it as a failure.

The previous-day rule also compounds: a three-day run earns two under it and one here.

Balance, not a counter

Comp-off earned is always derived from the OD entries, never stored as a running number. A stored counter can drift away from the days that justify it; a derived one cannot. The engine returns the earning blocks, not just a count, so the UI can say which weekends earned them — "17–18 Jan, 14–15 Feb" is an answer, "3" is a number.

Three further properties, each deliberate:

  • It is a running balance, not an annual one. Unlike annual leave it is never bounded by the leave year, matching the workbook, which sums it with no year bound and carries an opening figure.
  • It is not bounded by today either. A comp-off approved for next Monday is a decision already made. Bounding by the clock once showed "balance 1" for somebody who had already spent it.
  • A weekend block straddling New Year is attributed to the year of its last day, the day the entitlement completes. Because comp-off is a running balance this is a consistency rule rather than a policy.
Working a public holiday earns nothing back. Only both days of one weekend do. The seeded label for OD said "weekend or holiday work" until 31 August 2026, which promised staff a day the rule never granted.
Source: src/hr/lib/hr-comp-off.ts

5. Annual leave

Carry-over cap30 daysPositive only.
Negative balanceCarries in fullNever floored, never written off.

The calendar-year finding overturned the original plan, and it came from reading the workbook's formulas rather than its numbers: entitlement is IF(EDATE(joining,12) <= TODAY(), 30, 0), and the annual-leave COUNTIFS has no lower date bound. An anniversary-based accrual model would have been internally consistent and wrong.

The carry-over asymmetry is the policy, not an oversight. A positive carry-over is capped at 30. A negative one carries in full and is never clamped, because capping a debt would forgive it — and that would cancel the companion ruling that leave taken in advance follows the employee into the new year. Four people in the live data carry a negative balance. A Math.max(0, …) anywhere in this module is a bug.

The agreed override

A leaver's final year is usually negotiated rather than calculated. Setting Annual entitlement on the employee record replaces the standard 30 and is marked agreed on the summary so nobody later reads it as a mistake.

  • It beats the first-year gate, because a human decision should not be overruled by a rule.
  • It does not beat the employment window: it cannot make a year the person was never employed in worth anything.
  • Empty and zero are different. Empty means "use the rule"; zero means "we agreed on none".

6. Calendar days versus working days

When a range is recorded, does it charge every day in the block or only working days? The answer differs by category, and it was decided from the data rather than from a principle.

CategoryChargesWhy
Annual leaveCalendar daysA holiday booked Monday the 6th to Friday the 17th costs 12, not 10. The person was away.
On duty, comp-offCalendar daysThese describe the day itself rather than an absence from it. A Saturday is exactly when they belong.
Sick leave, everything elseWorking days onlyThe workbook recorded it that way.

The evidence: of the imported rows, 86 of 419 annual-leave days fall on a weekend, while only 2 of 45 sick days do. So the workbook charged annual leave across a block and recorded sick leave on working days. Applying the calendar rule to sick leave would have invented a policy nobody has.

Both recording paths must agree. A company shutdown booked as annual leave costs the same whether it was dragged on the grid or created as a standing rule. Until 31 August 2026 it did not — the rule path resolved weekends and holidays before the rule, so the same two-week shutdown cost 10 as a rule and 12 from the grid. That is why a calendar-day rule now sits at step 3 of the precedence chain, above the holiday and the weekend.

7. Sick leave

Article 31 of Federal Decree-Law 33/2021, per leave year: 15 days full pay, then 30 at half pay, then 45 unpaid.

The system warns; it never converts. Which tier somebody is on is which code was recorded. Recording a 16th SL-F day stops and shows how many have been used, what this would make it, and offers SL-H instead. Full pay can still be recorded deliberately. Nothing is written until a human chooses.

Automatic conversion was considered and rejected: the grid would show SL-F while payroll paid half, and the record would stop being the thing you can trust. That property is what the whole module rests on.

Two details in the check itself, each of which would be silently wrong if omitted:

  • Half days weigh 0.5. Counting days rather than weight would warn on six half-days as though they were six full ones, blocking a legal write.
  • It subtracts what is being overwritten. Otherwise re-saving a day that is already sick reads as adding a new one.

A tier's remaining figure may go negative, when more days were coded to a tier than it holds. That is a real data problem — somebody picked the wrong code — and it is shown rather than hidden.

8. Standing rules

A standing rule is a statement like "the company is remote from 12 to 23 March" or "Jinan works from home, permanently". It has a scope (ORG or EMPLOYEE), a date range, a leave code, and the organiser's own words for why.

A rule stores no days at all.

This is the second-biggest idea in the module after exception-only storage, and the numbers again are why. Of 386 imported work-from-home days:

  • 252 were twelve company-wide dates on which 20–22 people were all remote together (Ramadan, and the week after Eid).
  • 120 belonged to one permanently remote employee.
  • 14, across five people, were genuinely one-off.

So 386 rows were holding 27 facts. The other 359 existed only because the schema had no way to say "the company" or "always". Creating a rule writes nothing per person and deleting one removes nothing, which is what makes a rule reversible.

Rules reach the balance. A rule can carry any leave code, so a company-wide shutdown booked as annual leave has to reach payroll. If it did not, one record would quietly give twenty-three people free annual leave with nothing on screen to show it. Both balance paths — one employee, and the org summary — expand rules through the same resolver.

Which rule wins when two overlap

  1. Time not worked beats working — regardless of scope. This is what puts the permanently remote employee on leave during a company shutdown.
  2. Then employee scope beats organisation scope — the narrower statement.
  3. Then the later start date.

And an explicit entry always beats every rule, as step 2 of the precedence chain.

9. The leave-year roll

On 1 January the closing annual balance becomes next year's carried-in figure, capped in one direction. This is the piece the workbook has no equivalent for, because it only ever holds one year and the previous one arrives as a number somebody typed.

The roll writes a LeaveGrant row per employee still employed. It runs every night through January, not once, because it is an idempotent upsert that recomputes from the rows — so a December leave day entered on 5 January corrects the grant by itself.

The seed is never overwritten. Employee.carryoverDays and openingSickUsed are the figures typed in at go-live, and they belong to one leave year (seedLeaveYear). From the first roll onwards the carried-in figure lives on the grant. Overwriting the seed would make last year's closing balance unrecomputable the moment it was written, and a December day entered in January could never be reconciled. Non-destructive is what makes the roll re-runnable.

10. Edge cases worth knowing before you touch this

  • A year the person was not employed in gives zeros, and says so. The employment window helper returns null for such a year, and null means nothing, not no bound. Treating it as an open bound once summed a leaver's entire history against a fresh 30-day entitlement.
  • The first-year gate is judged at the earlier of today and the exit date. Somebody who left after eleven months never completed a year and must not flip to 30 days the day the calendar passes an anniversary they never reached.
  • 29 February. Someone who joined on 29 February has no anniversary three years in four. It is resolved in one place, by clamping to the last real day of the intended month, because Postgres, JavaScript and a human all answer differently.
  • The exit date is the last working day and is inclusive.
  • Calendar dates are YYYY-MM-DD strings, never Date objects. getDay() answers in the reader's timezone; a string does not move. Columns are @db.Date.
  • Every day count is a decimal, rounded to one place. Half days are the only fraction admitted, and a float sum of thirty 0.5s is not 15.
